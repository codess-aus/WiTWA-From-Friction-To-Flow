## The spine of the talk

**Michelle's fear isn't water — it's losing control.** The mask over her face, the enclosed space, the memory of the dentist putting her to sleep as a child. That is exactly what many people fear about AI: not the technology, but handing over control.

Every slide answers that fear the same way: **you stay in control.**

**The visual journey:** the diver in deep water → the submarine she steers → the rusty doors on the seabed → orbit → the bridge → the agentic loop → the crew → the astronaut on the moon → the wall of ideas → the team → the launch pad → the lit-up world → the astronaut facing that world, with things growing at her feet.

## Slide 1 — From Friction to Flow (~2 min)

### Script

*[Walk to your mark. Don't speak yet. Let the slide sit for 3 seconds, then gesture to the diver.]*

Look at this little diver.
Helmet sealed. Heavy suit. Every breath coming through a tube.

*[pause]*

I need to confess something. **That picture is my worst nightmare.**

Not the water. I surf. I swim. I love the ocean.

**It's the mask.** The first time I tried diving, the moment that mask went over my face, I was genuinely terrified. It took me straight back to being a kid at the dentist, the mask coming down to put me to sleep. **The enclosed space. The loss of control.**

So yes, **I put my worst nightmare on my opening slide.** Baby steps.

*[let the laugh land]*

But I chose this picture for a reason. Because I think that's how a lot of people feel about AI right now. **It's not the technology that scares us. It's the feeling of losing control.**

[TRIM] And when we feel like that, good ideas stay on the shore.

**Most ideas don't die from a lack of talent. They drown in friction.**

But look again. **This diver isn't sinking. They're swimming up.** Toward the light.

*[warmth, smile]*

Good afternoon, Women in Tech WA! I'm Michelle Sandford. I lead developer engagement for Azure at Microsoft across Australia and New Zealand. Which is a fancy way of saying **I spend my days helping people turn "I wonder if…" into "Look what I built."**

And here's what I've learned: **brave isn't the absence of fear. Brave is going in anyway.**

[TRIM] The best way to learn is by building. And right now, the distance between an idea and a first version has never been shorter.

This is not a talk about AI. **AI is not the hero of this story. You are.**

Because **the future isn't built by the people who felt ready. It's built by the people who started anyway.**

So let's start with the most important thing I'll say today…

*[Click]*

## Slide 3 — Friction is where good ideas go quiet (~2 min incl. show of hands)

### Script

*[Click. Lower your voice slightly, because we've gone deeper.]*

Down here, at the bottom of the deep, is where ideas end up.

Look at that light, behind the doors. That's an idea. **It's still glowing. Nobody switched it off. It just got stuck.**

Friction isn't a lack of talent. **It's the distance between intention and action.**

And it usually looks like one of these doors.

*[one beat per door, point to each word]*

**Time.** "I'll get to it after this quarter."
**Access.** "I don't have the tools. Or the permission."
**Language.** Everyone's saying API and LLM and SDLC, and you're nodding along.
**Process.** Fourteen steps between your idea and anyone actually seeing it.
And **confidence.** "Who am I to build this?"

*[pause]*

Notice something. **Confidence is only one of five doors.** So if your idea is stuck, **it's very often not you. It's the doors.**

*[AUDIENCE MOMENT]*

Let's find out. Quick show of hands. **Who here has an idea waiting behind one of these doors?**

*[wait for the hands]*

Keep your hand up if it's been waiting more than a year.

*[look around slowly]*

Look around this room. **That's a room full of ideas, glowing in the deep.**

And here's the thing about those doors. Look at the rust. **Doors don't rust from being opened. They rust from waiting.**

*[pause]*

So let's start with the heaviest door. Confidence.

*[Click]*

### Lines to land hard

1. **"It's still glowing. Nobody switched it off. It just got stuck."** — Hope, just before the hands go up.
2. **"It's very often not you. It's the doors."** — The line for this audience. Many women in tech have been taught to see friction as a personal failing; this says it's mostly the system. Expect nods.
3. **"That's a room full of ideas, glowing in the deep."** — Say it while hands are still up.
4. **"Doors don't rust from being opened. They rust from waiting."** — The most quotable line on this slide.

### How it ties together

- **Answers slide 2's question** ("what is stopping us?") straight away.
- **The glowing light behind the doors** returns on slide 9: "This is what it looks like when it gets out."
- **The show of hands escalates:** "Who has an idea?" gets almost every hand; "more than a year?" leaves the honest ones up. Look at the room, not your notes.
- **"More than a year"** is called back on slide 12: "You've had the idea for long enough."
- **"The heaviest door, confidence"** hands off to slide 4.

## Slide 4 — Confidence is not certainty (~1½ min)

### Script

*[Click. Pause for a beat. The jump from the seabed to space should feel big — let the room take it in.]*

So, the heaviest door. Confidence.

And look where we are now. Not at the bottom of the ocean. Not even at the surface. **We're in orbit.**

*[pause]*

Astronauts have a name for what happens up here. They call it **the overview effect.** You look back at the planet, the borders disappear, and everything that felt enormous on the ground suddenly looks small.

**From up here, you can't see a single one of those doors.**

That's what confidence really is. **Not certainty. Perspective.**

*[shift tone: practical, grounded]*

But here's the part nobody tells you. **Nobody launches straight from the seabed into space.** You don't get here in one brave leap.

You get here in loops. *[trace the loop with your hand]* **Try. See what happens. Learn. Try again.**

[TRIM] A draft gives you something to question. A prototype gives you something to improve.

**Every loop gives you a little more perspective,** until the things that stopped you start to look small.

*[gesture to the pink rings around the planet]*

And look at those rings, because there's a bit of physics I love. **An orbit is just falling.** Falling forward so fast that you keep missing the ground. Over and over.

So every try that didn't land perfectly? **That isn't you failing. That's you staying in orbit.**

*[pause, point to the words on the slide]*

Confidence is not certainty. **Confidence is a next step, plus feedback.**

And what if that loop could spin faster than ever before?

*[Click]*

### Lines to land hard

1. **"From up here, you can't see a single one of those doors."**
2. **"Not certainty. Perspective."**
3. **"Nobody launches straight from the seabed into space."** — Stops it sounding like a pep talk.
4. **"That isn't you failing. That's you staying in orbit."** — The one people will write down.

### How it ties together

- **From slide 3:** "the heaviest door" leads straight in. The doors felt huge on the seabed; from orbit you can't see them.
- **The confidence loop** (Try / See / Learn / Try again) returns on slide 5 ("this is what makes it spin faster") and slide 6 ("it's our confidence loop, just with a few more steps").
- **The rings around the planet** move to circle the astronaut on slide 8.
- **Hands off to slide 5** with "what if that loop could spin faster?"
- **Facts:** the overview effect is a well-documented shift in perspective many astronauts describe, including borders seeming to disappear. "Falling forward fast enough to keep missing the ground" is the standard plain-English explanation of an orbit.

## Slide 5 — AI is not the hero. It is the bridge. (~2 min)

### Script

*[Click. Settle the energy. We've come down from orbit, so this should feel grounded and practical.]*

Perspective is wonderful. But at some point, **you have to come back down to earth and build something.**

And on the ground, the gap still looks like this. You on one side. Your idea on the other. And in between, deep water. You could dive it. Mask on. In the dark.

*[beat, then a knowing smile]*

**You know how I feel about that.**

*[let the laugh land]*

At the start I told you AI is not the hero of this story. Here's what it actually is. **It's the bridge.**

It doesn't drain the ocean. The gap is still real. **But you don't have to dive it anymore.**

*[point to the wireframe on the right cliff]*

Now look at what's waiting on the other side. It's not a finished building. It's a frame. A wireframe. **A first draft.**

That's the promise. Not perfection. **A first version you can actually stand in, and react to, and improve.**

*[plain, practical tone]*

So when I say GitHub AI, **I'm not asking anyone here to learn a programming language.** I mean tools like GitHub Copilot and AI agents that help you explore a problem, make a plan, and draft something real. [TRIM] For some of you, that's code. For others, it's a journey map, a requirements list, a test scenario, a prototype.

And here's the mindset shift. **Don't treat it like an oracle.** An oracle gives you answers and expects you to believe them.

**Treat it like a fast first-draft partner.** A partner gives you a draft and expects you to argue with it.

*[slow down, this is the line]*

**Don't ask AI to be right. Ask it to be fast. You be right.**

Remember the loop? Try, see, learn, try again. **This is what makes it spin faster.** Minutes to a first draft, instead of weeks.

But a bridge only matters if it takes you somewhere. So what does that journey actually look like?

*[Click]*

### Lines to land hard

1. **"You know how I feel about that."** — Calls back your confession; by now the room is on your side, so expect a warm laugh.
2. **"It doesn't drain the ocean. But you don't have to dive it anymore."** — Honest about AI: the hard part is still there; you just have a way across.
3. **"An oracle gives you answers and expects you to believe them. A partner gives you a draft and expects you to argue with it."**
4. **"Don't ask AI to be right. Ask it to be fast. You be right."** — The strongest line on the slide.

### How it ties together

- **From slide 4:** "Perspective is wonderful, but you have to come back down" explains the jump from orbit back to the cliffs.
- **Back to slide 1:** you promised "AI is not the hero, you are" — the slide title now says it. The room will notice you kept your word.
- **Back to slide 2:** the submarine and the bridge are the same idea. Optional line: "First it was a submarine. Now it's a bridge."
- **The wireframe** returns on slide 7 (the Builder agent) and slide 9 ("A wireframe on the cliff").
- **"You be right"** sets up slide 8: "AI can propose. Humans decide what matters."
- **Hands off to slide 6** with "what does that journey look like?"
- "Minutes instead of weeks" is a figure of speech, not a measured claim.

## Slide 6 — The agentic SDLC: from intent to learning (~2¼ min)

### Script

*[Click. Disarm the acronym straight away, with a light, slightly playful tone.]*

This is the one slide with an acronym on it. **SDLC.** The software development lifecycle. Please don't let it scare you, **because you already know it.**

*[pause, open your arms to the room]*

**You're sitting inside one right now.**

*[walk the loop with your hand, one stage per beat]*

Somebody had an **intent**: bring the women in tech of WA together. They made a **plan**. They **built** it: speakers, venue, sessions. They **tested** it, with run sheets and rehearsals. They **reviewed** it. They **secured** it: your registration details, your safety in this room. And this morning, they **shipped** it. *[gesture to the organisers — let the room applaud them]*

And tomorrow? They'll **learn**. What worked, what didn't, what to do better next year.

*[beat]*

Intent, plan, build, test, review, secure, ship, learn. **That's not a software process. That's how anything worth building gets built.**

And look at the shape. **It's a loop.** Look familiar? It's our confidence loop. Try, see, learn, try again. Just with a few more steps.

*[shift: now explain "agentic"]*

So what does "agentic" add?

An agent is AI that can take on **a bounded piece of work across several of these steps.** It can look at the context it's allowed to see, make a plan, use the tools it's allowed to use, produce something, check its own work, and report back.

Which means **the loop spins faster.** Not just one first draft. A first plan, a first set of tests, a first security check.

*[point to the centre of the slide, slow down]*

But look at the middle of this slide. **People set direction.**

Remember what scares me? Losing control. **That's why this is in the middle.**

We've been in space today, so let me put it this way. **A rocket can fly itself. Mission control still decides where it's going.**

**Agentic does not mean hands-off. It means your hands are free for the parts only you can do.** Deciding what matters. Setting the boundaries. Saying "yes, ship it" or "no, not yet."

*[pause]*

**The agents carry the work. You carry the purpose.**

So let's meet the crew.

*[Click]*

### Lines to land hard

1. **"You're sitting inside one right now."** — The line that makes the acronym click for non-coders. Pause after it.
2. **"That's not a software process. That's how anything worth building gets built."** — Tells everyone in the room they're already builders.
3. **"Remember what scares me? Losing control. That's why this is in the middle."**
4. **"A rocket can fly itself. Mission control still decides where it's going."**
5. **"Agentic does not mean hands-off. It means your hands are free for the parts only you can do."** — Answers the fear of being replaced.
6. **"The agents carry the work. You carry the purpose."** — The line people will quote.

### How it ties together

- **From slide 5:** "What does that journey look like?" is answered by the loop.
- **Back to slide 4:** "It's our confidence loop, just with more steps" ties the human message to the technical one.
- **Back to slide 1:** "People set direction" is the direct answer to your fear of losing control.
- **To slide 7:** "Let's meet the crew" keeps the space theme and introduces the agents as crew members.
- **Shout-out moment:** gesturing to the organisers at "they shipped it" usually earns them applause — and wins you the room.
- WiTWA examples are kept general so nothing is inaccurate. If you know a real number (speakers, sessions), one specific figure lands even harder.

## Slide 7 — Agents are teammates with a job (~2 min)

### Script

*[Click. Light, energetic. You're introducing the crew.]*

On a space mission, nobody does everything. **Every member of the crew has a job.**

And that's the best way to think about AI agents. **Not a magic button. A teammate, with a job.**

Here's a crew you could put together tomorrow.

*[point to each card in turn]*

A **Researcher**, who gathers the context it's allowed to see and surfaces what matters.

A **Planner**, who turns a fuzzy goal into clear steps you can actually review.

A **Builder**, who drafts a first version you can react to. Remember the wireframe on the cliff? **That's this one.**

And my favourite. The **Tester**. Who asks what breaks, *[pause]* and for whom.

*[slow right down]*

**For whom.** Those might be the two most important words on this slide.

Because for decades, **car crash-test dummies were built around the average man.** It worked. *[beat]* **Just not for everyone.**

"It works" has too often meant **"it works for the people who built it."** A good testing agent asks the question that gets missed. [TRIM] What about someone on a slow connection? Using a screen reader? On their phone, on the bus?

*[point to the pink line at the bottom, pick the pace back up]*

And down here are the three rules for every crew member. **Defined jobs. Allowed access. Visible work.**

**You wouldn't give a new starter the keys to the whole building on day one. Don't give an agent the keys either.**

And **if a teammate won't show you what they did, they don't get the job.** Same goes for agents.

*[pause]*

But notice what's missing from this crew. **Nobody on it decides what matters.**

That job is still ours.

*[Click]*

### Lines to land hard

1. **"Not a magic button. A teammate, with a job."**
2. **"Crash-test dummies were built around the average man. It worked. Just not for everyone."** — The moment for a Women in Tech audience. Expect a reaction, and wait for it.
3. **"'It works' has too often meant 'it works for the people who built it.'"**
4. **"You wouldn't give a new starter the keys to the whole building on day one."** — "Allowed access" in one image anyone can picture.
5. **"Nobody on this crew decides what matters. That job is still ours."**

### How it ties together

- **From slide 6:** "Let's meet the crew" leads straight into "every member of the crew has a job."
- **Back to slide 5:** "Remember the wireframe on the cliff? That's this one" connects the Builder to the bridge image.
- **"Defined jobs. Allowed access. Visible work."** are the agent-level version of slide 2's "a window you can see through, and controls you keep your hands on" — you stay in control.
- **Plants the crash-test dummy,** which returns on slide 8 ("Who isn't this dummy?"), slide 9 ("all over again") and slide 11 ("good enough for whom?").
- **To slide 8:** "That job is still ours" leads into "AI can propose. Humans decide what matters."
- **Fact:** it's well documented that crash-test dummies were modelled on the average male body for decades, leaving women less protected. If you want to name a source, Caroline Criado Perez's *Invisible Women* is the one this audience is most likely to know.

## Slide 8 — AI can propose. Humans decide what matters. (~2½ min)

### Script

*[Click. Pause and let the image sit. Warm, slower, more personal.]*

Remember that little diver from my very first slide? Deep water, heavy suit, looking up at the light?

*[gesture to the astronaut]*

**Look where she ended up.**

And yes, **she's wearing a helmet. My other worst nightmare.**
But look at her face. **She's not trapped. She's looking up.**
**Because she knows who's in control.**

Standing on her own two feet. And look at those rings. On the confidence slide, they circled the planet. **Now they circle her.**

Because in everything we've talked about today, the bridge, the loop, the crew, **this is what it all orbits around. You.**

*[point to the headline]*

**AI can propose. Humans decide what matters.**

There are three questions no AI can answer on your behalf.

**Purpose.** What is this actually for?
**Boundaries.** What must it never do?
And **judgment.** Is this actually good enough? *[beat]* And good enough **for whom?**

*[callback, slow down]*

Remember the crash-test dummy? You could run a million perfect tests on that dummy, and every single one would pass. **It took a human to ask the real question. Who isn't this dummy?**

That's judgment. And it comes from people. The person who notices the accessibility issue. The person who sees who's missing from the data. The person who knows what actually happens in the real world, not just in the plan.

*[pause, this is your thesis]*

Here's what I believe. **When producing gets cheap, judgment gets precious.**

AI makes it easier to produce more. That doesn't make human judgment less important. **It makes it more important than it has ever been.**

And that means **the more AI can do, the more it matters who is in the room.**

*[look out at the audience, slowly]*

Look around. This room. Your experience. Your perspective. The questions you ask that nobody else thought to ask.

**That isn't a nice-to-have in the age of AI. That's the whole point.**

*[pause]*

So let me show you what that looks like in real life.

*[Click]*

## Slide 9 — The idea that became tangible (~2¼ min)

### Script

*[Click. Bring the energy up, this is a happy image. Gesture to the wall.]*

Remember the light, stuck behind those rusty doors? **This is what it looks like when it gets out.**

*[storytelling voice, a little softer]*

Let me paint you a picture.

Picture a community organiser. She wants to help people who are new to tech find a mentor. She has the insight. She has the relationships. What she doesn't have is a product team, or a spare six weeks.

So the idea sits behind the doors. Time. Access. Process.

Until one evening, she opens a conversation with AI and types one sentence. *"Help me map the journey from wanting a mentor to having a useful first conversation."*

And within the hour she has something she didn't have before. A journey map. A set of questions. A rough first version.

**Not finished. Not perfect. A wireframe on the cliff.**

And then she does the bravest thing. **She shows it to real people.**

*[point to the quotes, one at a time — give each its own beat]*

And real people react.

One says, *"The language feels intimidating."*
Another says, *"I need a mobile-friendly option."*
And then someone says, *"Not everyone has a network yet."*

*[pause]*

That last one stops her. **Because her whole idea quietly assumed people already had one.**

**That's the crash-test dummy, all over again.** And it took a human in the room to see it.

*[slow down]*

Here's the thing. Her first version didn't prove the idea was finished. **It made learning possible.**

**Nobody can give you feedback on an idea that lives in your head.** An idea in your head stays safe. **An idea on the wall gets better.**

*[point to the italic line on the slide]*

The breakthrough was not the finished product. **It was having something to react to.**

That's what a first draft really does. **It turns private confidence into shared progress.**

And notice who made it better. Not the AI. **The people around the wall.**

Which tells you something important about flow…

*[Click]*

### Lines to land hard

1. **"This is what it looks like when it gets out."** — Pays off the light behind the doors.
2. **"And then she does the bravest thing. She shows it to real people."** — The confidence lesson in action.
3. **"That's the crash-test dummy, all over again."** — Third use; now the audience recognises it.
4. **"An idea in your head stays safe. An idea on the wall gets better."**
5. **"It turns private confidence into shared progress."** — The best line on this slide.
6. **"Not the AI. The people around the wall."** — Hands straight to slide 10.

### How it ties together

- **Back to slide 3:** the glowing light that was stuck behind the doors is now out on the wall, with people around it.
- **Back to slide 5:** "A wireframe on the cliff" — the first draft in action.
- **Back to slide 7:** the crash-test dummy, all over again — a blind spot only a human in the room caught.
- **"An idea on the wall gets better"** returns on slide 12.
- **To slide 10:** "Not the AI. The people around the wall… which tells you something about flow."

### Swap in a real story (recommended)

The story above is framed honestly as an illustration ("picture a community organiser"). A true story from your own work or community will hit much harder. Use these beats:

1. **The person and the idea** — who they were and what they wanted to change
2. **The door** — what stopped them (time, access, language, process or confidence)
3. **The first draft** — the moment AI helped them get something tangible, and what that artefact was
4. **The reaction** — one or two real pieces of feedback, ideally one that exposed a blind spot
5. **What changed** — what happened because the idea got out of their head

"Within the hour" is part of the illustrative story — use the real timeframe if you swap.

## Slide 10 — Flow is a team sport (~2 min incl. show of hands)

### Script

## Slide 11 — Build bravely. Build with guardrails. (~2 min)

### Script

*[Click. Grounded and steady. This is the "trust" slide.]*

Can I tell you what it was that I actually feared about diving?

**Not the water. The loss of control.**

And here's what I learned. **Divers don't go deep because they're fearless. They go deep because they trust the system that brings them back.** Check your gear. Never dive alone. Come up slowly.

**That's what gives you control back.**

*[gesture to the rocket]*

Look at this rocket. Nobody thinks that launch tower slows it down. **The tower is the reason it gets to launch at all.**

**Build bravely. Build with guardrails.**

Responsible building isn't the opposite of bold building. **It's what lets bold building last.**

*[beat, a little playful]*

Why do cars have brakes? *[pause]* **So they can go fast.**

*[walk through the four cards]*

So here are the guardrails.

**Start small.** Use reversible experiments. Test in the shallow end first.

**Keep human approval,** especially where the impact is high. Before every launch there's a go or no-go call. Every controller, one at a time, has to say "Go." **Agents can do the work. A human still says "Go."**

**Protect data.** Behind every record is a real person who trusted you with it.

And **make quality visible.** Test for security, accessibility, bias and reliability. Remember the crash-test dummy. Ask, every time, **good enough for whom?**

*[slow down]*

Because the question was never only "Can AI do this?"

It's "Should it? Under what conditions? **And who reviews the result?**"

*[point to the pink line on the slide]*

**Speed without trust is not flow.** *[beat]* **It's just a faster way to crash.**

*[pause, then lighter energy]*

So. What can you actually do on Monday?

*[Click]*

### Lines to land hard

1. **"Not the water. The loss of control."** — Names the real fear, plainly.
2. **"Divers don't go deep because they're fearless. They go deep because they trust the system that brings them back."**
3. **"That's what gives you control back."** — Reframes guardrails as freedom, not restriction.
4. **"The tower is the reason it gets to launch at all."**
5. **"Why do cars have brakes? So they can go fast."**
6. **"Agents can do the work. A human still says 'Go.'"**
7. **"Speed without trust is not flow. It's just a faster way to crash."** — Links to the crash-test dummy without spelling it out.

### How it ties together

- **Back to slide 1:** your fear returns — now as a lesson about trust and control rather than fear.
- **Back to slides 6 and 8:** the go/no-go call shows "People set direction" and "Humans decide what matters" in action.
- **Back to slide 7:** "Good enough for whom?" is the last crash-test callback. Don't use it again after this, or it loses its punch.
- **Plants "Never dive alone,"** which returns on slide 12 ("Invite a friend").
- **To slide 12:** "What can you actually do on Monday?" turns the room towards action.
- **Facts:** "never dive alone" (the buddy system) and "come up slowly" are core scuba safety rules. The go/no-go poll before launch, each controller answering in turn, is standard NASA practice.

## Slide 13 — The close (~2 min)

### Script

*[Click. Don't speak straight away. Let the image land for a full 3 seconds. Slow, warm, deliberate.]*

We started today at the bottom of the ocean. A little diver, in a mask. **My worst nightmare.**

We went past the rusty doors. Up into orbit. Across the bridge.

*[gesture to the astronaut]*

**And here she is.** Standing on her own two feet, looking at that lit-up world.

*[beat]*

And yes, **she's still wearing the helmet.**

I'll be honest with you. I still don't love a mask over my face. **The fear didn't disappear. She just stopped letting it decide.**

*[point to the glowing plants]*

And look at her feet. **Things are growing.** Small things. First versions. That's where confidence comes from. **Not before you start. Along the way.**

*[step forward. Talk to the room, not the slide.]*

So here's what I want you to take home.

**You do not need to know everything before you begin.**

**You do not need to become someone else before your idea deserves a first version.**

**And you do not need to build alone.**

Bring the question. Bring the context. Bring the care.

**Use AI to fill the blank page. And use your judgment to decide what belongs on it.**

*[slow right down, this is your last big line]*

Because AI is going to change how we build. **But it doesn't get to decide who builds what's next.**

*[pause]*

**We do.**

*[point to the words on screen]*

The confidence to build what's next is not the price of admission. **It's what we build, together, along the way.**

*[beat. Smile.]*

**Go build what's next.**

Thank you.

### Lines to land hard

1. **"The fear didn't disappear. She just stopped letting it decide."** — Closes your personal story honestly, without pretending you've overcome it.
2. **"Things are growing. Not before you start. Along the way."** — Turns the slide's message into something the room can see.
3. **"You do not need…"** ×3 — a slightly longer pause each time.
4. **"AI doesn't get to decide who builds what's next. We do."** — The line for a Women in Tech room. It may get applause mid-close; if it does, wait for it to finish.
5. **"Helmet on. Hands on the controls. Go build what's next."** — Putting on the thing you fear, while staying in control.

### How it ties together

- **The diver** (slide 1) **is now the astronaut**, and the lit-up world she was swimming towards is in front of her.
- **Your fear of losing control** (slide 1) becomes **"Hands on the controls."**
- **"Brave is going in anyway"** (slide 1) becomes **"She just stopped letting it decide."**
